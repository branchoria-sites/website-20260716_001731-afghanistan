## Introduction

Clusters of students suddenly fainting, shaking, crying, hyperventilating or appearing to enter trance-like states have been reported in Philippine schools for decades. They are often described locally as cases of "possession", especially when several pupils are affected in rapid succession. Medical investigations, however, frequently conclude that these episodes are examples of **mass psychogenic illness** (also called mass sociogenic illness): real physical and psychological symptoms that spread through a group without evidence of a shared infectious disease, toxin or other physical cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40537604/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Factors related to the occurrence of mass psychogenic illness in schools: a systematic review - PubMedJune 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 19, 2025</span></span></span>

These incidents matter because they sit at the intersection of health, religion, education and culture. In a country where belief in spirits and Christian ideas about demonic influence coexist with modern medicine, different explanations can exist side by side. Understanding why these outbreaks spread helps explain both the distress experienced by students and the challenges faced by teachers, parents, doctors and religious leaders.

## What does mass psychogenic illness mean?

Mass psychogenic illness (MPI) refers to the rapid spread of genuine symptoms among members of a closely connected group without an identifiable physical cause. The symptoms are not deliberately invented or faked. Those affected experience real fear and real bodily reactions, even though laboratory tests and environmental investigations usually fail to identify poisoning, infection or another shared medical condition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40537604/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Factors related to the occurrence of mass psychogenic illness in schools: a systematic review - PubMedJune 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 19, 2025</span></span></span>

In school outbreaks around the world, common symptoms include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* fainting or dizziness;
* trembling or shaking;
* hyperventilation;
* crying or screaming;
* temporary weakness;
* trance-like behaviour or apparent loss of awareness.

</div>

In the [Philippines]({{ 'philippines/' | relative_url }}) these symptoms are often interpreted through religious language. Students, relatives or bystanders may believe an evil spirit has entered the victim, while health professionals may diagnose a dissociative or psychogenic episode. These explanations need not reflect bad faith on either side; they arise from different ways of interpreting the same observable behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1390009224813268480?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cir.nii.ac.jp">[CiNii Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cir.nii.ac.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ci Nii Research Reportage of “Possession” in the Philippines &#124; Ci Nii Research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ci Nii Research Reportage of “Possession” in the Philippines &#124; Ci Nii Research</span></span></span>

## How fear and observation amplify symptoms

School outbreaks provide an unusually clear demonstration of how emotions spread through tightly connected groups.

Research on school-based mass psychogenic illness consistently identifies several reinforcing factors:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* **High stress levels.** Academic pressure, examinations, family problems or emotional strain increase vulnerability.
* **Observation of classmates.** Seeing a friend collapse or panic can make similar symptoms more likely in others.
* **Rumours.** Stories about ghosts, curses or dangerous substances provide a framework for interpreting ambiguous sensations.
* **Authority responses.** Conflicting messages from teachers, health officials and community leaders can either calm or intensify fears.
* **Media and social media.** Dramatic reporting or viral videos may unintentionally reinforce expectations and prolong outbreaks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40537604/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Factors related to the occurrence of mass psychogenic illness in schools: a systematic review - PubMedJune 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 19, 2025</span></span></span>

</div>

This process does not imply that the symptoms are imaginary. Fear can trigger rapid physiological changes, including altered breathing, muscle tension, dizziness and fainting. Once these reactions become visible, they may spread through social observation, especially among adolescents in close-knit classrooms.

## The Bohol outbreak and competing explanations

One of the best documented recent Philippine examples occurred in **September 2023** at San Jose National High School in Talibon, Bohol.

During a school Mass, students began trembling, fainting and displaying behaviour that many witnesses interpreted as spirit possession. Some reported feeling an unseen force or seeing dark figures. The episode spread rapidly through the school, leading to class suspensions and hospital treatment for many pupils. Local reports estimated that around 250 students were affected, although not all required hospital care.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mb.com.ph/2023/9/16/panic-grips-high-school-in-bohol-as-250-students-possessed-by-evil-spirits" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mb.com.ph">[Manila Bulletin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mb.com.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manila BulletinManila Bulletin - Fear grips high school in Bohol as 250 students &#x27;possessed&#x27; by evil spirits...</span></span></span>

The incident quickly produced two competing narratives.

Medical staff at Garcia Memorial Provincial Hospital and local government officials described the outbreak as **mass hysteria** or **mass psychogenic illness**, noting that such events commonly affect anxious adolescents in group settings and that no common physical illness had been identified. Officials also urged the public not to spread alarming rumours that could worsen the situation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mb.com.ph/2023/9/16/panic-grips-high-school-in-bohol-as-250-students-possessed-by-evil-spirits" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mb.com.ph">[Manila Bulletin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mb.com.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manila BulletinManila Bulletin - Fear grips high school in Bohol as 250 students &#x27;possessed&#x27; by evil spirits...</span></span></span>

At the same time, many students, parents and community members understood the experience through a supernatural framework. Reports of unseen figures, unusual strength or altered voices reinforced beliefs that spirits or demonic forces were involved. These accounts circulated widely on social media and in local conversations, illustrating how cultural expectations shape the meaning attached to unusual events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mb.com.ph/2023/9/16/panic-grips-high-school-in-bohol-as-250-students-possessed-by-evil-spirits" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mb.com.ph">[Manila Bulletin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mb.com.ph</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manila BulletinManila Bulletin - Fear grips high school in Bohol as 250 students &#x27;possessed&#x27; by evil spirits...</span></span></span>

Later reports described similar episodes in other Bohol schools, again producing a mixture of medical explanations and spiritual interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theboholtribune.com/2023/12/10/evil-spirit-possessionplagues-bohol-schools/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theboholtribune.com">[Bohol Tribune]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theboholtribune.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bohol Tribune&#x27;Evil spirit&#x27; possessionplagues Bohol schools</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bohol TribuneDecember 10, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why possession narratives remain persuasive

The Philippines is one of the world's largest predominantly Christian countries, while many communities also retain older traditions concerning spirits, sacred places and unseen forces. Because of this religious landscape, unusual behaviour is more readily interpreted as possession than it might be in societies with different cultural expectations.

A study examining Philippine newspaper reporting argues that apparent possession in schools has become a recurring media theme despite receiving relatively little systematic attention from psychology or anthropology. News coverage often presents dramatic eyewitness accounts while giving comparatively less space to discussion of psychogenic illness, making possession narratives especially memorable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1390009224813268480?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cir.nii.ac.jp">[CiNii Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cir.nii.ac.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ci Nii Research Reportage of “Possession” in the Philippines &#124; Ci Nii Research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ci Nii Research Reportage of “Possession” in the Philippines &#124; Ci Nii Research</span></span></span>

Religious authorities themselves are not always in agreement. Catholic exorcists have sometimes discussed the possibility of genuine spiritual phenomena while also emphasising that psychological assessment and medical examination should come first. This reflects the Catholic Church's longstanding practice of distinguishing possible mental illness from cases considered appropriate for exorcism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cebudailynews.inquirer.net/528008/exorcist-explains-why-some-students-get-possessed-by-evil-spirits-in-schools" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cebudailynews.inquirer.net">[Cebu Daily News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cebudailynews.inquirer.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cebu Daily News Exorcist explains why mass possession happen in schools</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cebu Daily News Exorcist explains why mass possession happen in schools</span></span></span>

## What schools and health authorities have learned

Experience from the Philippines and comparable outbreaks elsewhere suggests that the response can influence whether an episode expands or subsides.

Measures commonly recommended include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* ensuring students receive prompt medical evaluation;
* ruling out environmental hazards before assuming psychological causes;
* reducing crowding around affected students;
* providing calm, consistent communication;
* offering counselling and emotional support;
* discouraging sensational rumours and unverified social media claims.

</div>

A recent systematic review of school outbreaks found that rumours, psychological stress, perceived threats, distrust of authorities and extensive media attention repeatedly contribute to the spread of mass psychogenic illness. These findings closely match patterns seen in Philippine school incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40537604/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Factors related to the occurrence of mass psychogenic illness in schools: a systematic review - PubMedJune 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 19, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why these outbreaks matter beyond individual schools

School possession scares reveal how collective belief, stress and observation interact rather than fitting neatly into either "supernatural" or "medical" categories. They demonstrate that cultural beliefs influence how symptoms are understood without proving or disproving supernatural explanations.

For historians and social scientists, these outbreaks offer insight into the relationship between religion, education and community anxiety in modern Philippine society. For educators and health professionals, they underline the importance of treating affected students with compassion while avoiding responses that unintentionally reinforce fear or stigma.

Rather than dismissing participants as irrational, the strongest evidence suggests that these episodes involve genuine distress shaped by social relationships, expectations and local beliefs. That combination explains why school possession scares continue to recur in the Philippines and why they remain among the country's most recognisable examples of collective fear and mass psychogenic illness.
